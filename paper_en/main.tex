\begin{abstract}
The One-to-one SkewGRAM problem is a complex combinatorial optimization challenge arising in bioinformatics, aiming to identify conserved metabolic and genomic signatures. It requires finding a path in a directed acyclic graph (representing metabolic pathways) such that its vertices induce a connected subgraph in an undirected graph (representing genomic interactions). Previous approaches using Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have shown promise but often struggle with finding long paths or require computationally expensive post-processing. In this paper, we propose a novel approach inspired by Natural Language Processing (NLP), specifically the Skip-Gram model. We transpose this technique to biological graphs, using a two-stage decoding process and structural negative sampling. Our method generates valid consistent paths significantly faster than Exact Solvers (ILP), achieving an average speedup of 68x, while offering a competitive alternative to existing GNN approaches.
\keywords{Combinatorial Optimization \and Natural Language Processing \and Skip-Gram \and Biological Networks \and Graph Algorithms.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Artificial Intelligence and its applications are increasingly relevant to bioinformatics, particularly in developing countries where these technologies offer the potential for rapid transformation. A key challenge in this field is the integration of multiple biological networks to extract meaningful functional modules. For instance, cellular metabolism can be modeled as a directed graph where biochemical reactions form arcs between metabolites. Simultaneously, physical interactions between genes or proteins can be modeled using undirected graphs.

The One-to-one SkewGRAM problem addresses the integration of these two levels of information by seeking paths that are consistent across two different graphs: a directed graph (causal processes) and an undirected graph (functional associations). Identifying such paths allows for the discovery of functional modules conserved throughout evolution.

Recent advances have applied Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) to this problem, but these methods remain limited either by the short length of the paths found (averaging 1.2 nodes) or by the need for heavy post-processing. In this work, we propose to improve upon the GNN approach with a faster NLP-based (Natural Language Processing) method. By adapting the Skip-Gram model (popularized by word2vec and DeepWalk), we tackle this specific combinatorial constraints problem.

\section{Problem Formulation}
The One-to-one SkewGRAM problem can be formally defined as follows:

Let $V = \{v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n\}$ be a set of nodes (biological entities).
\begin{itemize}
    \item $D = (V, A)$ is a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) representing directional biological processes.
    \item $G = (V, E)$ is an undirected graph encoding functional interactions.
\end{itemize}

The objective is to find a path $P = (v_1, \dots, v_k)$ in $D$ such that:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Path condition}: $(v_i, v_{i+1}) \in A$ for all $i$, meaning $P$ is a valid directed path in $D$.
    \item \textbf{Connectivity condition}: The subgraph of $G$ induced by the vertices $\{v_1, \dots, v_k\}$ is connected.
\end{enumerate}

Such a path is called \textbf{(D,G)-consistent}. Finding the longest (D,G)-consistent path is an NP-hard problem. Exact solvers like Integer Linear Programming (ILP) provide the optimal length but suffer from exponential execution times on large graphs.

\section{Proposed NLP Approach: Skew-GRAM}
Our approach is inspired by the successes of NLP in representation learning, translating graph nodes into "words" and random walks on $D$ into "sentences".

\subsection{Node Embeddings and Skip-Gram}
Similar to DeepWalk or node2vec, we generate sequences of nodes via random walks on the directed graph $D$. We then learn embedding matrices $\mathbf{V}_{in}, \mathbf{V}_{out} \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times d}$ ($d=32$). 
However, unlike the standard Skip-Gram, we introduce \textbf{structural negative sampling}.

The loss function is optimized as follows:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(c,o,n_{1:K}) = -\log\sigma(\mathbf{v}_{in,c}^\top \mathbf{v}_{out,o}) - \sum_{k=1}^K \log\sigma(-\mathbf{v}_{in,c}^\top \mathbf{v}_{out,n_k})
\end{equation}

The structural negatives $n_k$ are drawn from the non-successors of the target node $c$ in $D$ ($P_{struct}(n\mid c) \propto \mathbf{1}[(c,n)\notin A]$). This forces the latent space to strongly penalize impossible transitions in the causal graph, thereby rigorously capturing the structure of the DAG.

We also apply a sub-sampling of "hubs" (highly connected nodes) to prevent very dense paths from dominating the transition probabilities.

\subsection{Two-Stage Decoding}
Since the connectivity condition in $G$ applies to the \emph{final} set of vertices in the path (which may induce a tree, and not necessarily a sequential path in $G$), it is impossible to guarantee connectivity incrementally.
We therefore use a two-stage decoder:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Stage A (Generation)}: Beam search (width 4) on the successors of $D$, masking already visited nodes. The selection probability is guided by the similarity of the learned embeddings:
    $$P_{skew}(v\mid u,\mathcal{U}) = \frac{\mathbf{1}[(u,v)\in A]\cdot\mathbf{1}[v\notin\mathcal{U}]\cdot \exp(\mathrm{sim}(u,v)/\tau)}{\sum_{v'\in N^+(u)\setminus\mathcal{U}}\exp(\mathrm{sim}(u,v')/\tau)}$$
    \item \textbf{Stage B (Connectivity Filtering)}: On the candidate path (of length $L$), we apply a sliding window. For each contiguous sub-interval, we verify if the set of vertices induces a connected graph in $G$ using an incremental Union-Find algorithm in $O(L^2\cdot\alpha(L))$. We thus extract the longest $(D,G)$-consistent sub-path.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Experimental Results}
We evaluated our approach on a set of synthetic Erdős-Rényi instances (around 100 nodes per instance), which model the characteristics of biological networks. 
We compared the path lengths and execution times of our Skew-GRAM method with the ILP2 exact solver.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{figures/03_evaluation_comparison.png}
\caption{Left: Comparison of path lengths per instance (ILP2 vs Skew-GRAM). Right: Quality/time trade-off, demonstrating the high speed of Skew-GRAM.}
\label{fig:results}
\end{figure}

The results demonstrate that the Skew-GRAM method is \textbf{extremely fast}. While ILP2 takes an average of 28 seconds per instance, Skew-GRAM generates the path in 0.41 seconds, representing an \textbf{acceleration of over 68x}.
In terms of quality, the path generated during Stage A has an average length of 6.45. After the strict filtering of Stage B to ensure $(D,G)$-consistency, the average length is 3.42 (compared to 9.97 for the optimal ILP2 solution).
Although the method does not yet achieve the exact optimal length, it generates valid paths (unlike traditional Machine Learning methods such as Random Forest) and of a length far superior to the 1.2 nodes obtained by strict sequential GNN decoders.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
By treating metabolic and genomic networks as linguistic sequences and leveraging the Skip-Gram model with structural negative sampling, we have introduced a very fast heuristic for the NP-hard One-to-one SkewGRAM problem. This method positions itself as an excellent pre-processing filter (warm-start) for exact methods, outperforming traditional GNNs in terms of inference speed.

Future work will aim to directly integrate the connectivity of graph $G$ during the generation (Stage A) via Joint Embeddings, in order to reduce the path length gap with exact solvers.
