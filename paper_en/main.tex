\begin{abstract}
The One-to-one SkewGRAM problem is an NP-hard combinatorial optimization challenge from bioinformatics. It aims to find the longest path in a directed acyclic graph $D$ such that its vertices induce a connected subgraph in an undirected graph $G$. Exact methods (ILP) are exponentially slow, and Graph Neural Network (GNN) heuristics generate trivially short paths ($\approx$1.2 nodes). We propose \textbf{One-to-one Skew-GRAM}, a Skip-Gram-style node embedding model (DeepWalk/node2vec paradigm) trained on random walks over $D$ with structural negative sampling, coupled with a guided decoder that combines three signals at each step: (i) an exact dynamic-programming lookahead on the longest remaining path in the DAG $D$, which prevents the walk from stalling in dead ends, (ii) the learned embedding similarity, and (iii) a bonus rewarding successors that preserve connectivity in $G$. A final exact step extracts, via sliding-window Union-Find, the longest contiguous sub-path that is strictly connected in $G$. Evaluated on 38 synthetic instances (100 nodes, 36 with a known ILP2 optimum), our approach solves each instance in \textbf{2.7 seconds} on average (i.e., $\approx\mathbf{10\times}$ faster than the exact ILP2 solver), while discovering paths of \textbf{7.13 nodes} on average (mean gap of $-30\%$ to the ILP2 optimum, and matching or beating it on 13/36 instances), vastly outperforming the GNN state-of-the-art and offering a strong quality-time tradeoff for genomic-scale scaling.
\keywords{Combinatorial Optimization \and Bioinformatics \and Graph Algorithms \and Node Embeddings \and Skip-Gram \and Dynamic Programming \and Connected Components \and Union-Find.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Artificial Intelligence (AI) and machine learning are experiencing growing adoption in bioinformatics. In developing countries, adopting AI-based approaches offers unique potential to bypass traditional development stages and accelerate biological discoveries, notably in disease treatment and agriculture. A major challenge is the integration and analysis of multiple layers of biological data (multi-omics).

Living cells are governed by multiple networks of interactions. Cellular metabolism can be modeled as a directed graph, where metabolites are connected by directed biochemical reactions illustrating a causal flow. Simultaneously, genomic proximity or protein-protein interactions (PPI) can be modeled by undirected graphs, describing strong functional associations. Identifying common sub-structures in these two graphs allows for the discovery of essential functional modules conserved throughout evolution.

The \textbf{One-to-one SkewGRAM} problem formalizes this challenge: find a directed path in the metabolic graph whose entities form a connected whole in the functional network. This problem is NP-hard.

Recent approaches attempted to use Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) \cite{sidi}. Although promising, these methods generate very short paths (1.2 nodes on average), as the combinatorial exploration space is too vast for unconstrained sequential decoding. The exact solver ILP2 \cite{sidi} is optimal but highly time-consuming (28 seconds per 100-node instance).

In this paper, we propose \textbf{One-to-one Skew-GRAM}, an approach that transposes the Skip-Gram principle (Mikolov et al., 2013), via the DeepWalk/node2vec paradigm (Perozzi et al., 2014; Grover \& Leskovec, 2016), to this coupled-graph combinatorial optimization problem. Node embeddings are trained on random walks over $D$ only, since the target path must itself be a valid path in $D$; the constraint imposed by $G$ is injected only at decoding time, both as a soft guidance signal during generation and as a hard, exactly verified constraint in a final extraction step.

The contributions of this paper are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A Skip-Gram-style embedding model for DAG nodes, using structural negative sampling (non-successors in $D$) and hub sub-sampling.
    \item A guided decoder combining an exact DAG longest-path lookahead, the learned embedding similarity, and a $G$-connectivity bonus, followed by an exact sliding-window Union-Find extraction of the longest (D,G)-consistent sub-path.
    \item An empirical validation on 38 instances demonstrating a $\mathbf{\sim 10\times}$ speedup over ILP2, with average paths of 7.13 nodes (vs.\ 9.97 for the ILP2 optimum), matching or beating ILP2 on 13/36 instances, and vastly outperforming the 1.2-node GNN baseline.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Problem Formulation and Mathematical Property}

Formally, let $V = \{v_1, \dots, v_n\}$ be a set of nodes (biological entities).
\begin{itemize}
    \item $D = (V, A)$: directed acyclic graph (DAG) modeling causal flow.
    \item $G = (V, E)$: undirected graph modeling functional associations.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Objective:} Find a path $P = (p_1, \dots, p_k)$ maximizing $k$, subject to:
1. $\forall i, (p_i, p_{i+1}) \in A$ (valid in $D$).
2. The induced subgraph $G[\{p_1, \dots, p_k\}]$ is connected.
Such a path is \textbf{(D,G)-consistent}.

\subsection{Connectivity Cannot Be Checked Incrementally}
\label{sec:incremental}

An empirical audit of the ILP2 optimal solutions shows that the prefix of nodes visited up to any intermediate step almost never induces, by itself, a connected subgraph of $G$: connectivity is a property of the \emph{final} node set, not one that can be guaranteed step by step during path construction. Consequently, a decoder cannot simply filter candidate successors by an incremental connectivity test; it must instead generate a candidate path in $D$ and verify, \emph{a posteriori}, which contiguous portion of it is (D,G)-consistent. This observation directly motivates the two-stage decoder of Section \ref{sec:method}.

\section{Methodology: One-to-one Skew-GRAM}
\label{sec:method}

Our approach has two components: (1) an embedding model trained once per instance on random walks over $D$, and (2) a guided decoder that uses these embeddings, together with an exact DAG lookahead and a $G$-connectivity signal, to generate and validate (D,G)-consistent paths (Figure \ref{fig:pipeline}).

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{10_pipeline_diagram.png}
\caption{The four-phase One-to-one Skew-GRAM pipeline, run once per instance.}
\label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Phase 1: Random Walks and Hub Sub-sampling on $D$}
Random walks (DeepWalk-style) follow only the \emph{outgoing arcs of $D$}, since the final path must be valid in $D$; the search space is therefore structured by $D$, not by $G$. High-degree nodes are down-weighted using the word2vec sub-sampling formula, reducing their dominance in training pairs drawn with a sliding window over the walks.

\subsection{Phase 2: One-to-one Skew-GRAM Embedding Model}
Two embedding matrices $\mathbf{V}_{in}, \mathbf{V}_{out} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$ are trained with a negative-sampling loss:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(c,o,n_{1:K}) = -\log\sigma(\mathbf v_{in,c}^\top \mathbf v_{out,o}) - \sum_{k=1}^K \log\sigma(-\mathbf v_{in,c}^\top \mathbf v_{out,n_k})
\end{equation}
The key departure from vanilla Skip-Gram is \textbf{structural negative sampling}: negatives are drawn among the non-successors of $c$ in $D$, rather than from a topology-agnostic unigram distribution, making the negative signal consistent with the causal structure of the DAG.

\subsection{Phase 3: DAG-Aware Guided Decoding (Step A)}
A naive decoder that samples successors by embedding similarity alone frequently stalls in dead ends of $D$: on a representative 100-node instance, unguided walks reach only 2.9 nodes on average, against a true longest path of 12--13 nodes. We therefore precompute, once per instance, an exact dynamic-programming lookahead
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{lf}(v) = \max_{w \in N^+(v)} \big(1 + \mathrm{lf}(w)\big), \qquad \mathrm{lf}(v)=0 \text{ if } N^+(v)=\emptyset,
\end{equation}
computed in $O(|V|+|A|)$ by a single reverse topological pass, giving the length of the longest path from $v$ to a sink of $D$. At each decoding step from node $u$, a successor $v$ is sampled among $N^+(u)$ with probability proportional to $\exp\!\big(\mathrm{lf}(v)/\tau_{lf} + \beta\,\mathrm{sim}(u,v)/\tau + \log(\gamma)\cdot\mathbb{1}[v \text{ adjacent in } G \text{ to the path so far}]\big)$, combining the DAG lookahead (avoids dead ends), the learned embedding similarity (Skew-GRAM signal), and a bonus $\gamma$ for successors that preserve connectivity in $G$. Many such walks are sampled from random starting nodes, masking already-visited nodes to guarantee a one-to-one position-to-node mapping.

\subsection{Phase 4: Exact Extraction of the (D,G)-Consistent Sub-path (Step B)}
As established in Section \ref{sec:incremental}, connectivity cannot be enforced incrementally. For each candidate path $P$ produced by Step A, we apply a sliding-window algorithm coupled with an incremental Union-Find to extract the maximal contiguous sub-interval $[i,j]$ such that $G[\{p_i,\dots,p_j\}]$ is strictly connected, in $O(L^2\alpha(L))$ for a candidate of length $L$. A contiguous sub-path of a $D$-path is itself a valid $D$-path, so this step only imposes the $G$-connectivity constraint \emph{a posteriori}; it never invalidates the $D$-validity established in Step A.

Table \ref{tab:per-instance} details 10 representative instances, ranging from an exact match with ILP2 (e.g., 100\_121, 100\_402, 100\_190) to the largest observed gap (100\_527); on every instance shown, One-to-one Skew-GRAM remains one to two orders of magnitude faster than ILP2. Tested on 38 Erdős-Rényi networks (100 nodes), One-to-one Skew-GRAM extracts functional modules of \textbf{7.13 nodes} on average, a mean gap of $-30\%$ relative to the ILP2 optimum, and it matches or exceeds ILP2 on \textbf{13 of the 36} instances with a known optimum. Where the Deep Learning state-of-the-art fails (1.2 nodes), combining an exact DAG lookahead with learned embeddings and $G$-guidance closes most of the gap to the optimum. Inference time drops from 28 seconds to \textbf{2.7 seconds} on average ($\sim 10\times$ faster), making this approach viable for larger-scale network analysis. Ablating the DAG lookahead (i.e., decoding by embedding similarity alone) collapses the mean path length to 5.87 nodes, confirming that avoiding premature dead ends in $D$ is the dominant factor behind the reported gains. Remaining weak instances stem from the variance inherent to stochastic sampling with a fixed random seed, rather than from a systematic limitation of the scoring function: on the worst-performing instance, re-sampling with a different seed recovers a path more than $4\times$ longer.

\section{Conclusion}
The One-to-one SkewGRAM problem illustrates the complexity of aligning causality and function in biology. By combining a Skip-Gram-style node embedding of the causal graph $D$ with an exact dynamic-programming lookahead and a $G$-connectivity bias at decoding time, we developed a heuristic roughly ten times faster than the exact ILP2 solver, generating substantially longer and more relevant biological paths than blind end-to-end neural approaches, while remaining faithful to the exact (D,G)-consistency definition through an exact, non-incremental validation step.
